## Back-cover blurb (short hook, ~90 words)

Seventeen-year-old Brooke films everything, because a camera keeps what memory lets go. So when a sickness sweeps her town and her little sister walks out of the hospital cured, Brooke is the one who notices what no one else will: Claire came back wrong. She stands too close. She never wants a door shut between them. She remembers things she was never told. Everyone calls it love. Everyone tells Brooke to be grateful. And every day, the people who came home get a little better at pretending.

## Long description (retail, ~180 words)

Brooke Bennett has always trusted the camera more than the room. It keeps what people forget, and it does not lie the way faces do. So when an unexplained illness moves through her Connecticut town — filling the hospitals, emptying the desks at school, and then, impossibly, letting most of its victims recover — Brooke does what she always does. She records it.

Her sister Claire is one of the lucky ones. She comes home from Saint Vincent's pale, grateful, and desperate to be near the people she almost lost. It looks like relief. It looks like a family holding on after a scare. But Brooke keeps a list of the small, deniable things: the dog that won't come near Claire, the foods she used to hate, the way she waits outside a locked door as if a closed door were a kind of pain.

No one else sees it. The doctors, the school, the news, even Brooke's own mother keep repeating the same soft words about togetherness — until wanting to be left alone starts to feel like the dangerous thing.

A slow-burn horror novel about grief, surveillance, and the terrible distance between being loved and being kept. Book One.
