# Mortality and Grief Plan

Status: Approved design (author sign-off 2026-08-13)
Source issue: GitHub issue #80 ("Add depth")

## Purpose

Establish that the original illness kills before the changed survivors' behavior becomes recognizable, so that recovery feels miraculous, the public hunger for reunion and closeness reads as a human grief response, and coercive affection is harder to identify and resist. Deepen emotional and societal stakes without adding mythology, a competing subplot, statistics, or a mechanism for the illness.

## Approved decisions

- **Severity:** The acute illness kills a meaningful minority. Conveyed only through Brooke's limited experience (a confirmed personal death, empty desks, memorial language, hospital mortality, bereavement posts). No numbers dumped, no omniscient world reporting, no collapse spectacle. Institutions stay recognizable and functional with visible gaps.
- **Personal loss:** **Mr. Alvarez**, the crossing guard who is the subject of Brooke's two-minute application film (established Chapter 2). Confirmed death in the first wave. Not a disposable new character; he is the emotional center of her best work and ties grief directly to her documentation compulsion.
- **Nature:** Confirmed death. This keeps **Mark's silence as the only unresolved absence**, so the two do not blur.
- **When Brooke learns:** During the hospital sequence (Chapter 6), so mortality frames Claire's survival directly. Seeded earlier (Chapter 3) as community rumor and empty space.
- **Protected threads:** Mark's fate stays unknown. Claire and Sarah live. No biological specialness. No mechanism. The illness is never stated to "make people love more."

## Three layers (all through Brooke's POV)

### Global
The first wave visibly kills. Existing hospital overwhelm gains mortality signals: a covered gurney, staff counting overnight losses, ambulances that leave and do not return. Recovery is rare enough to feel like a reprieve.

### Community (Ashcroft)
- Empty desks and absent students at school.
- One classmate who got sick and did **not** recover, set against Claire, who did.
- Memorial language creeping into ordinary announcements and signage.
- Bereavement posts mixed into the survivor posts under Brooke's uploads.
- Public messaging (reunion, gratitude, "don't isolate") shown growing from collective grief, giving the institutional-capture layer an emotional root rather than a purely sinister one.
- Ordinary services continue with noticeable gaps.

### Personal — Mr. Alvarez
Brooke filmed him across three winter mornings for her application: warming a child's dropped mitten, checking an empty intersection twice, waving to a bus driver who never waved back. She never really spoke to him. The film earned her the workshop seat Sarah later cancels. He dies in the first wave. She holds the only footage that preserved his small, unwitnessed kindnesses.

## Cause-and-effect map (loss to later decisions)

| Established loss | Later decision it shapes |
|---|---|
| Alvarez dies; Brooke has the only record of him | Her recording of Claire becomes grief-driven, not just suspicious: the camera is how she keeps people she cannot afford to lose. Powers the compulsion that drives the uploads and the private archive. |
| People do not come back, but Claire did | Brooke clings to Claire's recovery as miraculous and excuses the early proximity signs longer, protecting the false-recovery ambiguity. |
| Sarah has watched the town lose people | Her fear of separation gains a grief root; her cancellation of the workshop and later containment read as love deformed by loss, not simple control. Complicates Brooke's rage with guilt. |
| Absence has already turned permanent (Alvarez, empty desks) | Mark's degrading contact carries real dread; Brooke keeps trying to reach him because she knows how fast silence becomes final. |
| Grief makes closeness feel morally owed | The community-wide rejection of isolation makes Brooke's need for space read as cruelty, raising the moral cost of separating from Claire and Sarah, and later making Anna's patient, undemanding safety more seductive. |

At least two of these (the documentation compulsion and the clinging to Claire's recovery) are load-bearing for the whole arc.

## Chapter insertion and revision list

- **Ch 2 Permission:** Light. Root Sarah's cancellation fear partly in the first deaths; keep the Alvarez film vivid as the seed.
- **Ch 3 Sisters:** Seed community mortality (an empty desk, a hushed name, the silent ambulance read as a hearse). First rumor that the crossing guard from Brooke's film is among the sick.
- **Ch 5 Empty:** Mortality present at the edges of the drive and the hospital approach (already partly there); a small concrete signal that people are dying, not only vomiting.
- **Ch 6 Red:** Brooke confirms Mr. Alvarez died. The hospital's mortality becomes personal; Claire's survival lands against his death. Core beat.
- **Ch 7 Better:** Consequence beat. Brooke returns to the Alvarez footage; the camera-as-preservation-of-the-dead theme crystallizes and reframes why she cannot stop recording Claire.
- **Ch 12 Everyone Is Fine:** School grief made visible (empty desks, memorial language, the counselor's reunion talk rooted in loss).
- **Ch 15 Somewhere Else:** Bereavement posts mixed into the viral survivor comparisons; the non-recovered classmate surfaces.
- **Ch 16-17:** Sarah's protectiveness and the institutional reunion language tied to accumulated loss.
- **Ch 22-25:** Grief raises the cost of separating; Alvarez and the empty places sharpen why leaving feels like risking permanent loss, and why Anna's promise of safety appeals.

## Guardrail compliance

- No new named character created only to die (Alvarez pre-exists; the non-recovered classmate is a light community touch, not a developed subplot).
- Mark's fate and the illness mechanism remain unresolved.
- Grief is never the sole reason danger is overlooked: denial, hope, institutional reassurance, and retained ordinary behavior still carry weight.
- The gradual progression, Anna mystery, and sequel-capable ending are preserved.

## Verification checklist

- Every death or absence produces a narrative consequence (no decoration).
- Claire's recovery reads as miraculous before it reads as wrong.
- The family's desire for closeness is emotionally credible.
- Reunion language grows from grief.
- At least two later Brooke decisions are materially shaped by the loss.
- Deaths conveyed through lived consequence, not exposition.
- No em dashes; commercial-prose and found-media rules intact.
