# Infection Model (Hidden)

> **AUTHOR REFERENCE ONLY. NEVER STATED, NAMED, OR CONFIRMED ON THE PAGE IN BOOK 1.**
> This is the private engine that keeps every on-page rule consistent and gives a sequel its runway. Book 1's reader assembles *behavioral* dread ("recovered is the wrong word, and I can't prove it") and is never handed the mechanism. If a character or the narration ever says "parasite," explains reject/accept, or confirms the biology, the book has failed.

## The model

The illness is a **compatibility-gated parasite** (host framework, not a virus in the ordinary sense; the route stays unknown on the page and undecided here on purpose).

1. A person becomes infected. The acute illness (fever, vomiting, dehydration, collapse) is the parasite establishing itself and the body deciding its verdict.
2. The verdict is binary. **Reject → the person dies.** **Accept → the person "survives."**
3. "Survives" is the trap. The accepted host is **occupied and impersonated.** The parasite wears the host and does an impression of the person. The person, for the purposes of this story, is gone.
4. What the parasite expresses, in every host, is an unbearable, boundaryless **need for closeness and possession.** It presents as love, grief, and devotion. That is why it hides so well and why the grieving public and institutions welcome it.

There is **no third outcome.** There is no cure and no safe recovery. Getting sick is a coin-flip between dying and coming back wrong.

## The red liquid (locked)

- The CDC/authorities rushed the red liquid into hospitals as an emergency treatment after the first deaths — within the opening days of the surge, consistent with the Prologue already showing it in hospital use. The exact origin and timing are never stated on the page, so no on-page reconciliation is required; keep the rollout early and statewide.
- It has **no bearing on reject or accept.** It does not decide, cause, or prevent the takeover.
- The "survivors" it appears to save were always going to accept. The treatment's apparent success is **coincidence and statistics**, dressed up as a cure.
- Everyone deploys and celebrates it in good faith. "Recovery rates improve." The dead are dismissed as "didn't get it in time." This ties the medicine to the institutional-capture theme (#77) and the grief theme (#80): a useless, comforting treatment that the public clings to because grief needs hope, whose "recoveries" are the takeovers.
- On the page it stays exactly as ambiguous as it already is (Prologue: "could have been the treatment... a pattern, not an answer"). Never confirm it is useless; let the reader assemble it.

## The Venom shorthand (private only)

Useful for us: host **compatibility** (incompatible burn out and die; compatible bond and survive) equals reject/accept. Three deliberate divergences keep this book itself, and the reader must never feel they are watching Venom:

1. **No coexistence.** There is no host still "in there" partnering with the thing. Acceptance is erasure wearing a mask. The flickers of the old self (Claire's beach tears, the "sorry, I didn't" after the wrist-grab) stay **ambiguous forever** — Brooke can never tell whether that is the real person surfacing or the parasite mimicking her. Ambiguity is more devastating than "trapped inside screaming," and it protects the ending (no one can be saved back).
2. **Hidden, not explained.** Venom names the alien in act one. This never does.
3. **Love, not power.** No strength, no appetite for spectacle. The "enhancement" is emotional coercion that looks like family.

## Hard guardrails

- **No protagonist or bloodline specialness.** Compatibility (who rejects, who accepts) is unexplained and must never resolve into Brooke or her family being immune, chosen, or special. That is a hard no.
- Never name it, never explain the route, never confirm the biology on the page.
- Mark's fate stays unknown (the one unresolved absence).
- Claire and Sarah remain accepted/changed; their arcs and final-act roles are intact. Sarah is the tragic hinge: genuinely grieving and protective *before* she is infected, then occupied, at a threshold no one can pinpoint.

## What Brooke and the reader are allowed to reach in Book 1

- **Behavioral only.** Brooke's best end-state understanding: the recovered are not who they were, "recovered" is the wrong word, closeness has become a cage, and she cannot prove any of it. She never suspects "parasite" — she is a teenage documentarian, not a scientist, and the reader should stay one step ahead of her but never certain.

## On-page seeds already present (fair-play for a future reveal)

These exist in the manuscript and are consistent with the model, so a sequel reveal would be earned, not retconned:

- The reject/accept binary rendered as **deaths vs. "recoveries"** with no true cures (Prologue, hospital sequence; sharpened by #80's mortality layer).
- The **uniformity** of the changed — every changed person wants the same things in the same ways (same parasite in every host).
- **Animals** refuse the changed (Cooper) — sensing the thing, not the person.
- The changed **cannot state direct falsehoods** (the parasite has not mastered the host's voice).
- The single, **non-repeatable green flicker** — a trace of the thing underneath, never clean proof.
- **Anna "learning how to love her"** — the parasite studying a host's bonds to wear her better.
- **"Recovery" celebrated by institutions** while the celebrated recoveries are the changed.

## Light on-page emphasis to add (never explaining)

Sharpen the sense that **"recovery" is a threshold some cross and some don't, and crossing it is the bad ending** — without naming why. Concretely: keep death and "recovery" side by side so the reader feels the coin-flip and the absence of a safe third outcome, and let the celebrated red-liquid "successes" quietly *be* the changed. Optionally, one or two **cold institutional breadcrumbs** a sequel can detonate (the recovered-vs-died numbers not behaving like an ordinary illness; the red liquid's rollout not moving the ratio) — planted, never resolved.

## Deferred to a sequel

- The parasite's origin, route, and ultimate purpose.
- Whether any accepted host retains anything.
- What the red liquid actually is.
- The wider outbreak's trajectory.
